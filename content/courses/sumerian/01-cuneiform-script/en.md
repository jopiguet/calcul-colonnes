# 1. Cuneiform and its transliteration

Writing was invented in southern Mesopotamia, in the city of Uruk, around 3300 BC, to keep the accounts of temples. The first signs were pictures drawn in clay; pressed with a cut reed, the lines became **wedges** (Latin *cuneus*), and the script became *cuneiform*. The language of the first texts is **Sumerian**, a language with no known relatives. This lesson shows how the script works and how Assyriologists write it in Latin letters, the transliteration used in every exercise of this course.

## From picture to wedge

| Sign | Origin | Values | Meanings |
|---|---|---|---|
| 𒀭 | a star | *an*, *diĝir* | sky; god |
| 𒆠 | a piece of land | *ki* | earth, place |
| 𒂍 | a house | *e₂* | house, temple |
| 𒀀 | running water | *a* | water |
| 𒅗 | a head with the mouth marked | *ka*, *inim*, *dug₄* | mouth; word; to speak |
| 𒁺 | a foot | *du*, *gin*, *gub* | to go; to stand |
| 𒈗 | a man with a crown: *lu₂* + *gal* | *lugal* | king, "big man" |

Around 2500 BC the signs were turned 90° to the left and written in lines from left to right. The script was used for three thousand years, for Sumerian, Akkadian, Hittite, Elamite and other languages; the last datable tablet is from about AD 75.

## One sign, several readings

A cuneiform sign can be read in several ways (**polyphony**): 𒀭 is *an* "sky" or *diĝir* "god". And several signs can have the same sound (**homophony**): there are many signs read *du*. Transliteration tells them apart with a number in subscript:

| Transliteration | Sign | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *du* | 𒁺 | to go |
| *du₃* | 𒆕 | to build |
| *dug₄* | 𒅗 | to speak |
| *e₂* | 𒂍 | house |
| *lu₂* | 𒇽 | man, person |

Older books write the first indices with accents: *dú* = *du₂*, *dù* = *du₃*. A sign written in capitals, KA, DU, is its **name**, used when its reading is uncertain.

## Conventions

| Convention | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| lower case: a Sumerian reading | *lugal* | king |
| hyphen: signs of one word or one chain | *e₂-gal* | palace, "big house" |
| subscript number: which sign of that sound | *du₃* | to build |
| superscript: a determinative, not read | ᵈ*en-lil₂* | the god Enlil |
| ĝ: the *ng* of *sing* | *diĝir* | god |
| š, ḫ | *šu*, *ḫe₂* | as in Akkadian: *sh*, Scottish *ch* |

The determinatives are signs placed before or after a word to show its class, like the Egyptian ones: ᵈ (*diĝir*) before gods, ᵍⁱˢ before wooden things, ˡᵘ² before professions, ᵏⁱ after places, ᵐᵘˢᵉⁿ after birds.

In the exercises, type š, ĝ, ḫ and the subscripts with the buttons, or as *sh*, *g*, *kh* and plain digits: *du3* is *du₃*.

::: summary
- Cuneiform: pictures pressed into clay with a reed, become wedges; Uruk, about 3300 BC; used for 3,000 years and many languages.
- One sign, several readings (𒀭 *an*, *diĝir*); one sound, several signs (*du*, *du₃*, *dug₄*).
- Lower case for readings, hyphens inside a word, subscripts for homophones, superscripts for determinatives (ᵈ, ᵍⁱˢ, ᵏⁱ), capitals for sign names.
:::
