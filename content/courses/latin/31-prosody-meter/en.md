# 31. Prosody and meter

Latin verse does not rhyme and is not built on stress, as English verse is. It is built on **quantity**: the alternation of long and short syllables. A line of Virgil is a pattern of longs (—) and shorts (∪), and reading it aloud with that pattern is called **scansion**. This last lesson teaches the rules for syllable length, the two most important meters, and scans the opening of the *Aeneid*.

## Long and short syllables

| Rule | A syllable is long when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| By nature | its vowel is long (macron) or a diphthong (*ae, au, oe, ei, eu*) | *Rō-ma*, *cau-sa* |
| By position | its vowel is followed by two consonants, even across a word boundary | *ar-ma*, *in-de*; *et stat* |
| Exceptions | a mute (*p, b, t, d, c, g*) + liquid (*l, r*) may leave it short | *pa-tris* or *pat-ris* |

*X* (= *cs*) and *z* count as two consonants; *h* does not count; *qu* is one consonant. The final syllable of a line is treated as long (*syllaba anceps*).

**Elision**: a word ending in a vowel, a diphthong or *-m* loses that final syllable before a word beginning with a vowel or *h*: *multum ille* is read *mult(um) ille*, *atque ea* as *atqu(e) ea*.

## The dactylic hexameter

The meter of epic (Ennius, Lucretius, Virgil, Ovid's *Metamorphoses*). Six feet:

| Foot | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pattern | — ∪ ∪ or — — | — ∪ ∪ or — — | — ∪ ∪ or — — | — ∪ ∪ or — — | — ∪ ∪ (almost always) | — — or — ∪ |

A **dactyl** is — ∪ ∪, a **spondee** — —. The fifth foot is nearly always a dactyl, which gives the line its recognizable close: *— ∪ ∪ — —*, as in *prīmus ab ōrīs*. A **caesura**, a word end inside a foot, usually falls in the third foot.

## The elegiac couplet

The meter of love poetry and epigram (Catullus, Tibullus, Propertius, Ovid's *Amōrēs*): a hexameter followed by a **pentameter**, two halves of — ∪ ∪ — ∪ ∪ — with a fixed pause in the middle. The first half may replace its dactyls with spondees; the second half never does.

| Line | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Hexameter | — ∪∪ · — ∪∪ · — ∪∪ · — ∪∪ · — ∪∪ · — — |
| Pentameter | — ∪∪ · — ∪∪ · — ‖ — ∪∪ · — ∪∪ · — |

## Reading: Virgil, Aeneid 1.1–4

*Arma virumque canō, Trōiae quī prīmus ab ōrīs* \
*Ītaliam fātō profugus Lāvīniaque vēnit* \
*lītora, multum ille et terrīs iactātus et altō* \
*vī superum, saevae memorem Iūnōnis ob īram.*

Of arms I sing and of the man who first came from the shores of Troy, exiled by fate, to Italy and the Lavinian shores; much was he tossed on land and on the deep by the power of the gods, because of the unforgetting anger of cruel Juno.

The first line, scanned:

| Foot | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Syllables | ar-ma vi- | -rum-que ca- | -nō Trō- | -iae quī | prī-mus ab | ō-rīs |
| Pattern | — ∪ ∪ | — ∪ ∪ | — — | — — | — ∪ ∪ | — — |

Line 3 elides *mult(um) ille*. *Superum* is an old genitive plural (*superōrum*), "of the gods above". *Lāvīnia* scans *Lā-vī-ni-a* with four syllables; *Ītaliam* has a long first *I* for the meter.

::: summary
- Long by nature (long vowel, diphthong) or by position (two consonants follow); mute + liquid may leave it short.
- Elision: final vowel or *-m* before a vowel or *h* is dropped.
- Hexameter: six feet of dactyls (— ∪ ∪) or spondees (— —); fifth foot a dactyl; caesura in the third foot.
- Elegiac couplet: hexameter + pentameter (— ∪∪ — ∪∪ — ‖ — ∪∪ — ∪∪ —).
- *Aeneid* 1.1: *ar-ma vi-rum-que ca-nō…*, D D S S D S.
:::
