# 17. Participles and the ablative absolute

Latin has three participles, and it uses them far more than English does: where English stacks clauses with "when", "because", "although", Latin often packs the same idea into one participle. This lesson adds the **present active** and the **future active** participles to the perfect passive you already know, then shows the most Roman construction of all, the **ablative absolute**.

## The three participles

| Participle | Formation | amō | regō | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Present active | stem + *-ns, -ntis* | amāns, amantis | regēns, regentis | loving, ruling |
| Perfect passive | supine stem + *-us, -a, -um* | amātus, -a, -um | rēctus, -a, -um | (having been) loved, ruled |
| Future active | supine stem + *-ūrus, -a, -um* | amātūrus, -a, -um | rēctūrus, -a, -um | about to love, going to rule |

The present participle is a third-declension adjective of one ending (like *ingēns*): *amāns, amantis, amantī, amantem, amante* (ablative in *-ī* when it is used as a plain adjective). Third *-iō* and fourth conjugations insert *-ie-*: *capiēns, capientis*; *audiēns, audientis*.

The participles do not give an absolute time; they give time **relative to the main verb**:

| Participle | Time relative to the main verb | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Present | at the same time | *Puerum clāmantem audīvī.* I heard the boy (while he was) shouting. |
| Perfect | before | *Urbem captam dēlēvērunt.* They took the city and destroyed it. |
| Future | after | *Moritūrī tē salūtant.* Those who are about to die salute you. |

## The ablative absolute

A noun and a participle in the **ablative**, grammatically detached from the rest of the sentence, form a small clause of their own: the **ablative absolute**. It gives the circumstances of the main action: time, cause, condition or concession. Translate it with a clause; the context chooses the conjunction.

| Latin | Literal | Natural English |
|---|---|---|
| *Urbe captā, hostēs fūgērunt.* | the city having been taken | When the city had been taken, the enemy fled. |
| *Caesare dūcente, vincēmus.* | Caesar leading | With Caesar in command, we shall win. |
| *Signō datō, mīlitēs impetum fēcērunt.* | the signal having been given | When the signal had been given, the soldiers charged. |
| *Cicerōne cōnsule* | Cicero (being) consul | in Cicero's consulship |

Latin has no present participle of *sum*, so two nouns, or a noun and an adjective, can stand alone: *Cicerōne cōnsule*, *mē invītō* (against my will), *Hannibale vīvō* (while Hannibal was alive).

Rule: the noun of an ablative absolute is normally **not** the subject or object of the main verb. When it is, Latin uses an ordinary participle instead: *Urbem captam dēlēvērunt*, not *urbe captā eam dēlēvērunt*.

::: summary
- Present active participle: *amāns, -antis* (same time); perfect passive: *amātus* (before); future active: *amātūrus* (after). Time is relative to the main verb.
- The present participle declines like *ingēns*; *-iō* and fourth conjugations have *-iēns*.
- Ablative absolute: noun + participle in the ablative, detached from the sentence; translate with "when", "because", "although", "with".
- Without a participle: *Cicerōne cōnsule*, *mē invītō*.
:::
