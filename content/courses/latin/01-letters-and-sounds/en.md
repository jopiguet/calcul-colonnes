# 1. Letters, sounds and stress

By the end of this lesson you can read any Latin word aloud the way an educated Roman of Cicero's time said it, and put the stress on the right syllable. Everything later builds on this: endings are recognised by ear as much as by eye.

## The alphabet

Classical Latin used 23 letters: A B C D E F G H I K L M N O P Q R S T V X Y Z. There was no J, U or W. The letter I stood both for the vowel *i* and for the consonant *y* (as in *yes*); V stood both for the vowel *u* and for the consonant *w*. K, Y and Z were rare, kept for a few old words and for Greek names.

Modern books differ in how they print these letters. This course writes **u** for the vowel and **v** for the consonant (*servus*, *vīta*), and **i** for both values of I (*iam*, *Iūlius*). You may type *j* or *u* for the consonants if your edition does: the answers accept both.

## Vowels: short and long

Latin has five vowels, each either short or long. A long vowel lasts about twice as long as a short one; the quality barely changes. Length can be the only difference between two words.

| Short | Long | Pair |
|---|---|---|
| a | ā | *malum* evil · *mālum* apple |
| e | ē | *venit* he comes · *vēnit* he came |
| i | ī | *liber* book · *līber* free |
| o | ō | *os* bone · *ōs* mouth |
| u | ū | *rosa* (nominative) · *rosā* (ablative) |

The Romans did not mark length in writing. Dictionaries and this course write a macron (ā) over long vowels in tables and in new words. You never need to type it: every answer is accepted with or without macrons.

Six **diphthongs** join two vowels in one syllable, and are always long:

| Diphthong | Said like | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ae | *ai* in *aisle* | *puellae* [pu-el-lai] |
| oe | *oi* in *oil* | *poena* [poi-na] |
| au | *ow* in *how* | *aurum* [au-rum] |
| ei | *ey* in *hey* | *deinde* [dein-de] |
| eu | *e-oo* run together | *heu* [heu] |
| ui | *wee* | *cui* [kwi] |

## Consonants

Most consonants sound as in English. The ones that differ are the ones that matter:

| Letter | Always | Example |
|---|---|---|
| c | [k], never [s] | *Cicerō* [ki-ke-rō] |
| g | hard [g], as in *go* | *genus* [ge-nus] |
| v | [w] | *vīnum* [wī-num] |
| i before a vowel | [y] | *iam* [yam] |
| s | [s], never [z] | *rosa* [ro-sa] |
| t | [t], even before *i* | *nātiō* [nā-ti-ō] |
| qu | [kw] | *quis* [kwis] |
| x | [ks] | *rēx* [rēks] |
| ch, ph, th | aspirated *k*, *p*, *t* (Greek words) | *philosophia* |

Double consonants are held longer: *annus* (year) is not *ānus*. In *gn* the *g* was nasal: *magnus* [mang-nus].

## Syllables

A word has as many syllables as it has vowels and diphthongs. A single consonant between two vowels goes with the second (*ro-sa*); two consonants are split (*pu-el-la*, *an-nus*), except a stop followed by *l* or *r*, which may stay together (*pa-tris*).

## Long and short syllables

The stress depends on whether the second-to-last syllable is long or short. A syllable is **long**:

- **by nature**, when its vowel is long or a diphthong: *a-mī-cus*, *Rō-mae*;
- **by position**, when its vowel, even short, is followed by two consonants (or by *x* or *z*, which count as two): *pu-el-la*, *ma-gis-ter*.

Otherwise it is **short**: *fē-mi-na* (the *i* is short and only one consonant follows).

## Where the stress falls

Latin stress follows two rules, and never falls on the last syllable:

1. A word of two syllables is stressed on the first: *RO-sa*, *AU-rum*.
2. A longer word is stressed on the second-to-last syllable (the **penult**) if it is long; otherwise on the one before it (the **antepenult**).

| Word | Syllables | Penult | Stress |
|---|---|---|---|
| *amīcus* | a-mī-cus | long by nature | a-**MĪ**-cus |
| *puella* | pu-el-la | long by position | pu-**EL**-la |
| *fēmina* | fē-mi-na | short | **FĒ**-mi-na |
| *dominus* | do-mi-nus | short | **DO**-mi-nus |
| *magister* | ma-gis-ter | long by position | ma-**GIS**-ter |

::: exercise stress
Which syllable carries the stress?

- *amīcus* → ((a / mī* / cus))
- *fēmina* → ((fē* / mi / na))
- *puella* → ((pu / el* / la))
- *dominus* → ((do* / mi / nus))
- *magister* → ((ma / gis* / ter))
- *agricola* → ((a / gri* / co / la))
:::

The last example needs a word on *agricola*: its penult *co* is short, so the stress goes back to *gri*: a-**GRI**-co-la.

## Reading aloud

Read these greetings and short sentences aloud, then check what they mean.

| Latin | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *Salvē!* | Hello! (to one person) |
| *Salvēte!* | Hello! (to several) |
| *Valē!* | Goodbye! |
| *Quid agis?* | How are you? |
| *Bene, grātiās.* | Fine, thanks. |
| *Ita vērō.* | Yes indeed. |

:::

::: summary
- 23 letters; I and V are both vowels and consonants (*i*/[y], *u*/[w]).
- Five vowels, short or long; long ones carry a macron in this course, never needed in answers.
- *c* and *g* are always hard, *v* is [w], *ae* is [ai].
- A syllable is long by nature (long vowel, diphthong) or by position (two consonants follow).
- Two syllables: stress on the first. More: on the penult if long, else on the antepenult.
:::
