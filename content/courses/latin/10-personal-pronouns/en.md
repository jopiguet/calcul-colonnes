# 10. Personal pronouns, the reflexive and possessives

Latin seldom needs a subject pronoun, since the verb says who acts. But "me", "to you", "with us", "himself" and "my", "your", "his own" are everywhere. This lesson gives the personal pronouns, the reflexive *sē*, and the possessive adjectives, with the one distinction English does not make: *suus* against *eius*.

## *Ego*, *tū*, *nōs*, *vōs*

| Case | I | you (sg.) | we | you (pl.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | ego | tū | nōs | vōs |
| Genitive | meī | tuī | nostrum, nostrī | vestrum, vestrī |
| Dative | mihi | tibi | nōbīs | vōbīs |
| Accusative | mē | tē | nōs | vōs |
| Ablative | mē | tē | nōbīs | vōbīs |

The nominative appears only for emphasis or contrast: *Ego labōrō, tū dormīs*, "*I* work, *you* sleep". The genitives are rarely "my, your": they serve as objects (*memor tuī*, mindful of you) or as partitives (*ūnus nostrum*, one of us). For "my", use the adjective *meus*.

*Cum* is attached to the end of these pronouns: *mēcum* (with me), *tēcum*, *nōbīscum*, *vōbīscum*.

## The reflexive *sē*

When an object of the third person refers back to the subject, Latin uses *sē*, which has no nominative and is the same in singular and plural:

| Case | himself, herself, itself, themselves |
|---|---|
| Genitive | suī |
| Dative | sibi |
| Accusative | sē (sēsē) |
| Ablative | sē (sēcum: with himself) |

*Mārcus sē laudat*: Marcus praises himself. *Mārcus eum laudat*: Marcus praises him (someone else). For the first and second persons, the ordinary pronouns are already reflexive: *mē laudō*, I praise myself.

## Possessive adjectives

| Person | Possessive | Example |
|---|---|---|
| my | meus, mea, meum | *liber meus* |
| your (of one) | tuus, tua, tuum | *māter tua* |
| his, her, its, their (own) | suus, sua, suum | *suōs amīcōs* |
| our | noster, nostra, nostrum | *patria nostra* |
| your (of several) | vester, vestra, vestrum | *domus vestra* |

They decline like *bonus* or *pulcher* and agree with the thing owned, not with the owner: *Iūlia frātrem suum amat*, Julia loves her brother: *suum* is masculine because *frātrem* is. The vocative of *meus* is *mī*: *mī fīlī!*, "my son!"

## *Suus* or *eius*?

*Suus* refers to the subject of its clause; *eius* (and *eōrum, eārum*) to anyone else.

| Latin | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *Mārcus librum suum legit.* | Marcus reads his (own) book. |
| *Mārcus librum eius legit.* | Marcus reads his (another man's) book. |
| *Puellae mātrem suam vocant.* | The girls call their (own) mother. |
| *Puellae mātrem eārum vocant.* | The girls call their (other girls') mother. |

::: exercise suus-eius
Choose *suus* (own) or *eius / eōrum* (someone else's).

- Julia sees her (own) brother: *Iūlia frātrem … videt.* → ((suum* / eius))
- Julia sees her (Clodia's) brother: *Iūlia frātrem … videt.* → ((suum / eius*))
- The soldiers praise their (own) king: *Mīlitēs rēgem … laudant.* → ((suum* / eōrum))
- The boys love their (own) mother: *Puerī mātrem … amant.* → ((suam* / eōrum))
- I know Marcus; I see his father: *patrem … videō.* → ((suum / eius*))
:::

::: summary
- *Ego, tū, nōs, vōs*: dative *mihi, tibi, nōbīs, vōbīs*; accusative *mē, tē, nōs, vōs*; *mēcum, tēcum…*
- Reflexive *suī, sibi, sē, sē*: refers to the subject of the third person.
- Possessives *meus, tuus, suus, noster, vester* agree with the thing owned; vocative *mī*.
- *Suus* = the subject's own; *eius, eōrum* = someone else's.
:::
