# 25. Gerund, gerundive and supine

English "-ing" does a lot of work: "the art of *speaking*", "a city *to be defended*", "he came *to ask*". Latin divides that work among three forms built on the verb: the **gerund** (a verbal noun), the **gerundive** (a verbal adjective, "to be …-ed"), and the **supine** (a rare verbal noun of purpose). The gerundive also gives one of the most frequent constructions in prose: the **passive periphrastic**, "must be done".

## The gerund

A neuter verbal noun of the second declension, singular only, with no nominative (the infinitive serves as subject). It is formed like the present participle with *-nd-*:

| Case | amō | regō | audiō | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Genitive | ama**ndī** | rege**ndī** | audie**ndī** | *ars amandī*, the art of loving |
| Dative | ama**ndō** | rege**ndō** | audie**ndō** | rare |
| Accusative | ama**ndum** | rege**ndum** | audie**ndum** | *ad regendum*, for ruling |
| Ablative | ama**ndō** | rege**ndō** | audie**ndō** | *discimus legendō*, we learn by reading |

*Causā* and *grātiā* follow a genitive gerund to express purpose: *pugnandī causā*, for the sake of fighting.

## The gerundive

A verbal adjective in *-ndus, -nda, -ndum*, declined like *bonus*, passive in meaning: *liber legendus*, "a book to be read". When the gerund would take an object, Latin prefers the gerundive agreeing with that noun:

| Gerund + object (possible) | Gerundive (preferred) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *ad urbem capiendum* | *ad urbem capiendam* | to take the city |
| *causā pācem petendī* | *pācis petendae causā* | to seek peace |
| *dē librōs legendō* | *dē librīs legendīs* | about reading books |

## The passive periphrastic

Gerundive + *sum* = obligation: "must be done". The person who must do it is in the **dative of agent** (not *ā* + ablative).

| Latin | Literal | Natural |
|---|---|---|
| *Carthāgō dēlenda est.* | Carthage is to be destroyed. | Carthage must be destroyed. |
| *Mihi epistula scrībenda est.* | The letter is to be written by me. | I must write the letter. |
| *Nōbīs pugnandum est.* | It is to be fought by us. | We must fight. |
| *Caesarī omnia erant agenda.* | Everything was to be done by Caesar. | Caesar had to do everything. |

An intransitive verb takes the neuter impersonal: *Eundum est*, one must go.

## The supine

The fourth principal part, in *-um* (accusative) after verbs of motion, expresses purpose; a rare ablative in *-ū* goes with some adjectives:

| Form | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *-um* | *Lēgātī vēnērunt pācem petītum.* | Envoys came to ask for peace. |
| *-ū* | *mīrābile dictū* | wonderful to say |
| *-ū* | *facile factū* | easy to do |

::: exercise translate
Translate.

- *Haec urbs nōbīs dēfendenda est.* → {{We must defend this city. / This city must be defended by us.}}
- *Lēgātī pācis petendae causā vēnērunt.* → {{The envoys came to seek peace. / The envoys came for the sake of asking for peace.}}
- *Dīcendō hominēs persuādēmus.* → {{We persuade people by speaking. / By speaking we persuade men.}}
- *Mīrābile dictū!* → {{Wonderful to say! / Amazing to tell!}}
:::

::: summary
- Gerund *amandī, -ō, -um, -ō*: verbal noun, no nominative; *causā* + genitive gerund = purpose.
- Gerundive *amandus, -a, -um*: passive verbal adjective; preferred to gerund + object.
- Passive periphrastic: gerundive + *sum* = must; agent in the dative (*mihi*); impersonal *eundum est*.
- Supine *-um* after motion = purpose; *-ū* with adjectives (*mīrābile dictū*).
:::
