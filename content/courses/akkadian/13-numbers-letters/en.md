# 13. Numbers and letters

Thousands of Old Babylonian letters survive: merchants, officials, family members writing to each other on small clay tablets. They open with the same formula and count with the same wedges. This lesson gives both: how numbers are written and said, and how a letter begins.

## Writing numbers

Two signs are enough: the vertical wedge 𒁹 for one and the corner wedge 𒌋 for ten. They are repeated and written tens first: 𒌋𒌋𒁹𒁹𒁹 is 23. The system is sexagesimal: after 59, the vertical wedge counts sixties, as minutes do after seconds. Texts usually write numbers this way, with signs, rather than in words.

| Signs | Value |
|---|---|
| 𒁹 | 1 |
| 𒁹𒁹𒁹 | 3 |
| 𒌋 | 10 |
| 𒌋𒁹𒁹 | 12 |
| 𒌋𒌋𒌋 | 30 |

## Saying numbers

| Value | With a feminine noun | With a masculine noun |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *ištiat* | *ištēn* |
| 2 | *šittā* | *šinā* |
| 3 | *šalāš* | *šalāšat* |
| 5 | *ḫamiš* | *ḫamšat* |
| 10 | *ešer* | *ešeret* |

From three to ten the gender is reversed: a masculine noun takes the form with *-at*: *šalāšat ūmū* "three days" (*ūmum* is masculine). The counted noun is plural, and stands after the number.

## The letter formula

| Akkadian | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *ana bēliya* | to my lord |
| *qibīma* | say: |
| *umma Awīl-Ninurta waradkama* | thus (says) Awil-Ninurta, your servant: |
| *Šamaš u Marduk liballiṭūka* | may Shamash and Marduk keep you in good health |

The tablet speaks to the scribe who reads it aloud: *qibīma* is "say!" (imperative of *qabûm* with *-ma*). *Umma* introduces whose words follow, and *-ma* closes the name.
