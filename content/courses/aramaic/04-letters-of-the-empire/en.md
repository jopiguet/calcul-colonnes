# 4. Letters of the empire

The Persian kings governed in Aramaic: orders, letters and accounts went in it from Persepolis to Egypt. This lesson reads the kings' names as the scribes wrote them, the places of the best-kept archive, and the greeting of a letter.

## The Persian kings

| Letters | Transliteration | King |
|---|---|---|
| 𐡊𐡅𐡓𐡔 | kwrš | Cyrus |
| 𐡃𐡓𐡉𐡄𐡅𐡔 | dryhwš | Darius |
| 𐡇𐡔𐡉𐡀𐡓𐡔 | ḥšyʾrš | Xerxes |
| 𐡀𐡓𐡕𐡇𐡔𐡎𐡔 | ʾrtḥšsš | Artaxerxes |

- The names are Persian: Old Persian *Kuruš*, *Dārayavauš*, *Xšayaršā*, *Artaxšaçā*. The scribes spelled them with Aramaic letters, using w and y for some of the vowels.
- The Bible keeps the same names in its Aramaic and Hebrew books: *Kōreš*, *Dāryāweš*, *ʾĂḥašwērōš*, *ʾArtaḥšaśtā*.
- Darius I had his deeds carved at Behistun in three languages; an Aramaic copy, on papyrus, was found in Egypt.

## Yeb, the fortress

| Letters | Transliteration | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 𐡌𐡑𐡓𐡉𐡍 | mṣryn | Egypt |
| 𐡉𐡁 | yb | Yeb, Elephantine |
| 𐡁𐡉𐡓𐡕𐡀 | byrtʾ | the fortress |
| 𐡌𐡓𐡀 | mrʾ | lord |

- On Elephantine, an island of the Nile at Aswan, a garrison of Judaean soldiers served the Persian king in the 5th century BCE. They called the place 𐡉𐡁 *yb*, *Yeb*, and wrote 𐡉𐡁 𐡁𐡉𐡓𐡕𐡀 *yb byrtʾ*, "Yeb the fortress".
- Their papyri are letters, contracts and accounts; among them are the Aramaic copy of Behistun and the story of Ahiqar, a wise scribe of the Assyrian court.
- In 407 BCE their leader Yedanyah and the priests wrote to Bagohi, governor of Judah, to have the temple of their god rebuilt; it had been destroyed three years earlier.
- 𐡌𐡓𐡀 *mrʾ*, *mārē*, is "lord". 𐡌𐡓𐡀𐡉 *mrʾy* is "my lord", 𐡌𐡓𐡀𐡍 *mrʾn* "our lord": the ending gives the owner.

## The greeting of a letter

𐡔𐡋𐡌 𐡌𐡓𐡀𐡍 𐡀𐡋𐡄 𐡔𐡌𐡉𐡀 𐡉𐡔𐡀𐡋

"May the god of heaven ask after the welfare of our lord": word for word, "the welfare of our lord, the god of heaven, may he ask". Letters to a superior open with such a wish; the writer then calls himself 𐡏𐡁𐡃𐡊 *ʿbdk*, "your servant".
