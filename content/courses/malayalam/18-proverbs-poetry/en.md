# 18. Proverbs, Ezhuthachan and Kumaran Asan

Malayalam literature is old: the language took its own shape around the 9th to 13th centuries, and its classics read like the language of today with an older polish. This last lesson goes from the proverbs everyone uses to two poets every Malayali learns at school.

## Proverbs (പഴഞ്ചൊല്ല്)

| Proverb | Reading | Word for word |
|---|---|---|
| മുറ്റത്തെ മുല്ലയ്ക്ക് മണമില്ല | *muṟṟatte mullaykkŭ maṇamilla* | the yard's jasmine has no scent |
| ആന കൊടുത്താലും ആശ കൊടുക്കരുത് | *āna koṭuttāluṁ āśa koṭukkarutŭ* | give even an elephant, but do not give hope |
| അണ്ണാൻ കുഞ്ഞും തന്നാലായത് | *aṇṇān kuññuṁ tannālāyatŭ* | even the squirrel's young: what it can |
| കാക്കയ്ക്കും തൻ കുഞ്ഞ് പൊൻകുഞ്ഞ് | *kākkaykkuṁ tan kuññŭ ponkuññŭ* | even to the crow its own chick is a golden chick |
| ഒരുമയുണ്ടെങ്കിൽ ഉലക്കമേലും കിടക്കാം | *orumayuṇṭeṅkil ulakkamēluṁ kiṭakkāṁ* | with unity one can sleep even on a pestle |

The jasmine in your own yard is the talent at home that nobody notices. The elephant: never raise hopes you will not fulfil, it is worse than any gift withheld. The squirrel comes from the Ramayana, where it helped build Rama's bridge by rolling in sand and shaking it off between the stones: every contribution counts. The crow's chick is every parent's child. The pestle is narrow: with unity, even hardship is bearable.

Lesson 19 gathers these and twenty-eight more in a compendium, all of them in the course vocabulary.

Note the forms: -ഉം "even" (കാക്കയ്ക്കും), -ആലും "even if" (കൊടുത്താലും), -എങ്കിൽ "if" (ഉണ്ടെങ്കിൽ), -അരുത് "must not".

## Ezhuthachan

Thunchaththu Ezhuthachan (16th century) is called the father of Malayalam: his *Adhyatma Ramayanam Kilippattu*, the Ramayana told by a parrot (കിളി *kiḷi*), fixed the literary language and made the script popular. Families read it aloud through the month of കർക്കടകം. It opens with the name of Rama:

ശ്രീരാമ! രാമ! രാമ! ശ്രീരാമചന്ദ്ര! ജയ \
ശ്രീരാമ! രാമ! രാമ! ശ്രീരാമഭദ്ര! ജയ

"Śrī Rāma! Rāma! Rāma! Śrī Rāmacandra, victory! Śrī Rāma! Rāma! Rāma! Śrī Rāmabhadra, victory!"

## Kumaran Asan: *Veena Poovu*

Kumaran Asan (1873–1924) opened modern Malayalam poetry with *Veena Poovu*, "The Fallen Flower" (1907): a flower that lies on the ground, once the queen of the plant, becomes a meditation on how all splendour passes. The first stanza:

ഹാ! പുഷ്പമേ, അധികതുംഗപദത്തിലെത്ര \
ശോഭിച്ചിരുന്നിതൊരു രാജ്ഞികണക്കയേ നീ \
ശ്രീ ഭൂവിലസ്ഥിര – അസംശയ – മിന്നു നിന്റെ- \
യാഭൂതിയെങ്ങു പുനരെങ്ങു കിടപ്പിതോർത്താൽ!

"Ah, flower! How you shone, high on your lofty seat, like a queen! Fortune on earth is fleeting, without a doubt: where is your splendour now, and where do you lie, when one thinks of it!"

| Word | Parts | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| പുഷ്പമേ *puṣpamē* | പുഷ്പം flower + vocative -ഏ | O flower |
| അധികതുംഗപദത്തിൽ | അധിക very + തുംഗ high + പദം place, locative | on a very high seat |
| രാജ്ഞികണക്കയേ | രാജ്ഞി queen + കണക്കേ like | like a queen |
| ഭൂവിലസ്ഥിര | ഭൂവിൽ on earth + അസ്ഥിര unstable | fleeting on earth |
| ഓർത്താൽ | ഓർക്കുക remember, think, + -ആൽ | if one thinks |

The poem is in a Sanskrit metre and full of Sanskrit words, as classical Malayalam poetry is; the grammar joining them (locative -ഇൽ, vocative -ഏ, -ആൽ) is the grammar of this course.
