# 5. The Anglo-Saxon Futhorc

The Angles, Saxons and Frisians took the runes to England and the coast of the North Sea, and went the other way from Scandinavia: they added runes. Old English had changed its vowels, and the row grew to 28 runes, then more in Northumbria. It was used from the 5th century to the 11th, beside the Latin alphabet brought by the Church.

## The futhorc

The fourth rune now sounded *o*, so the row is the *futhorc*: ᚠᚢᚦᚩᚱᚳ. Most of the old runes stay, some with new names, some with new shapes.

| Rune | Sound | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| ᚩ | o | *ōs* | god, or mouth |
| ᚳ | c (k) | *cēn* | torch |
| ᚻ | h | *hægl* | hail |
| ᛄ | j (y) | *gēr* | year |
| ᛇ | eo | *ēoh* | yew |
| ᛉ | x | *eolhx* | elk-sedge |
| ᛝ | ŋ (ng) | *ing* | the hero Ing |
| ᛟ | œ | *ēþel* | homeland |

And five runes at the end, for the new vowels:

| Rune | Sound | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| ᚪ | a | *āc* | oak |
| ᚫ | æ | *æsc* | ash tree |
| ᚣ | y | *ȳr* | bow (uncertain) |
| ᛡ | io | *īor* | a river creature (uncertain) |
| ᛠ | ea | *ēar* | earth, grave |

- ᚪ *a* and ᚫ *æ* are both forms of the old ᚨ; ᚩ *o* is a third. *æ* is the vowel of English *cat*.
- ᚻ has two bars where the Elder ᚺ had one.
- ᛉ keeps its shape but writes *x*, since the old *z* sound had gone.

## The Rune Poem

The Old English Rune Poem, perhaps of the 9th or 10th century, gives each rune a short stanza that begins with its name. The first line is about wealth: *Feoh byþ frofur fira gehwylcum*, "Wealth is a comfort to every man". It is one of the sources from which the names of the Elder Futhark are reconstructed.

Runes were also carved on great monuments. The Ruthwell Cross, in Scotland, carries lines of the poem *The Dream of the Rood* in runes; the Franks Casket, a whalebone box of the 8th century, mixes runes and Latin letters.
