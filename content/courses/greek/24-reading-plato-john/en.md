# 24. Reading: Plato, Heraclitus and John

Four short texts to end with, from four centuries of Greek: the god of Delphi, a philosopher of Ephesus, Plato's Socrates, and the opening of a gospel written in the common Greek, the *koinē*, of the Roman East. Each one uses grammar from this course; each has shaped European thought.

## The texts

| Source | Greek | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| Delphi | γνῶθι σεαυτόν. | Know yourself. |
| Heraclitus | ποταμῷ γὰρ οὐκ ἔστιν ἐμβῆναι δὶς τῷ αὐτῷ. | It is not possible to step twice into the same river. |
| Plato, *Apology* 38a | ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ. | The unexamined life is not worth living for a human being. |
| John 1:1 | Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος. | In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. |

## Notes

**γνῶθι** is the aorist imperative of γιγνώσκω (aorist ἔγνων, a root aorist like ἔστην). σεαυτόν "yourself" is σε + αὐτόν.

**ἔστιν** at the head of the clause means "it is possible" (lesson 7); ἐμβῆναι is the aorist infinitive of ἐμβαίνω "step in"; τῷ αὐτῷ, inside the article, is "the same" (lesson 13). The saying comes to us through Plutarch; Plato sums up the idea as πάντα ῥεῖ, "everything flows".

**ἀνεξέταστος**: ἀν- "not" + ἐξεταστός "examined", a verbal adjective in -τος, like βιωτός "livable", from βιόω "live". The dative ἀνθρώπῳ is "for a human". Socrates says it at his trial, after the verdict, refusing to stop his questioning.

**Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος**: ἦν is the imperfect of εἰμί. In θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος the article marks the subject: ὁ λόγος is the subject, θεός the predicate, placed first for emphasis.

::: summary
- γνῶθι σεαυτόν: root aorist imperative of γιγνώσκω.
- ὁ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ: verbal adjectives in -τος, dative "for".
- Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος: the article marks the subject; ἔστι + infinitive "it is possible".
:::
