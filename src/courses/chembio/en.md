# Chemical equations — the essentials of the test

## How to use this course

This course goes with the chemical equations test, which asks you to balance equations and reason with them. Read the parts in order: each builds on the one before.

| Level | What it asks |
|---|---|
| Beginner | What a coefficient and a subscript mean, why an equation must balance |
| Intermediate | Balancing simple equations: combustions, syntheses, decompositions |
| Advanced | Longer equations with polyatomic ions and many elements |
| Expert | Oxidation numbers, redox half-equations, limiting reactants |

## Part 1 — Reading an equation

In $\mathrm{2\,H_2 + O_2 \to 2\,H_2O}$, the reactants are on the left and the products on the right. A **subscript** belongs to the formula: $\mathrm{H_2O}$ has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. A **coefficient** counts molecules: $\mathrm{2\,H_2O}$ is two molecules, so four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms. States are written in brackets: (s), (l), (g), (aq).

## Part 2 — Balancing

Atoms are neither created nor destroyed, so each element appears the same number of times on both sides. Change only the coefficients, never the subscripts: that would change the substance.

1. Start with the element that appears in the fewest formulas, and leave hydrogen and oxygen for last.
2. Treat a polyatomic ion that stays whole, such as $\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}}$, as one unit.
3. Use a fraction if needed, then multiply everything to get whole numbers.
4. Check every element, then reduce to the smallest whole coefficients.

Example: $\mathrm{C_3H_8 + O_2 \to CO_2 + H_2O}$. Carbon gives $\mathrm{3\,CO_2}$, hydrogen gives $\mathrm{4\,H_2O}$, and the right side then has $6 + 4 = 10$ oxygen atoms, so $\mathrm{5\,O_2}$: $\mathrm{C_3H_8 + 5\,O_2 \to 3\,CO_2 + 4\,H_2O}$.

## Part 3 — Redox

An oxidation is a loss of electrons, a reduction a gain. Oxidation numbers keep the count: an element alone is 0, oxygen is usually −2, hydrogen +1, and the numbers in a species add up to its charge. In $\mathrm{KMnO_4}$, $+1 + x + 4(-2) = 0$ gives $x = +7$ for manganese.

To balance a redox equation in acid, write each half-equation, balance the atoms other than O and H, then O with $\mathrm{H_2O}$, H with $\mathrm{H^+}$, and the charge with electrons. Multiply so that both half-equations exchange the same number of electrons, and add them:

| Half-equation | Electrons |
|---|---|
| $\mathrm{MnO_4^- + 8\,H^+ + 5\,e^- \to Mn^{2+} + 4\,H_2O}$ | 5 gained |
| $\mathrm{Fe^{2+} \to Fe^{3+} + e^-}$ | 1 lost, so times 5 |

## Part 4 — Stoichiometry

The coefficients give the proportions in moles. With $n = \frac{m}{M}$, convert masses to moles, use the coefficients, and convert back. The **limiting reactant** is the one that runs out first: divide the moles of each reactant by its coefficient, and the smallest result limits. With $\mathrm{2\,H_2 + O_2 \to 2\,H_2O}$, 4 mol of $\mathrm{H_2}$ and 1 mol of $\mathrm{O_2}$ give $\frac{4}{2} = 2$ against $\frac{1}{1} = 1$: oxygen limits, and 2 mol of water form.
