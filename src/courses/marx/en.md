# Marx's Capital — a course in twelve parts

## How to read this course

*Capital* is Marx's critique of political economy: an account of how a society in which wealth takes the form of commodities produces, distributes and reproduces itself, and why that process is driven by the growth of value rather than by needs. This course follows the three volumes in order, explains each argument with numbers you can check, and points to the chapters to read. It does not ask you to agree with Marx; it asks you to understand what he claims, why, and where later readers disagree.

**The three volumes.**

| Volume | Published | Subject | Parts of this course |
|---|---|---|---|
| I. The Process of Production of Capital | 1867, by Marx (2nd edition 1872, French edition 1872–75) | Value, money, surplus value, the working day, machinery, wages, accumulation | 1–8 |
| II. The Process of Circulation of Capital | 1885, edited by Engels from Marx's manuscripts | The circuits of capital, turnover, the reproduction of the whole social capital | 9–10 |
| III. The Process of Capitalist Production as a Whole | 1894, edited by Engels | Profit, prices of production, the falling rate of profit, merchant capital, interest and credit, ground rent | 11–12 |

Volumes II and III are Engels's editions of drafts Marx left unfinished; the original manuscripts, published since in the *MEGA²* edition, show that Engels reorganised a good deal. Where that matters for an argument, this course says so.

**Notation.** The same letters are used throughout, as in most English editions.

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $c$ | constant capital: the value of the means of production used up (materials, energy, wear of machines) |
| $v$ | variable capital: the value of the labour-power bought, paid as wages |
| $s$ | surplus value: the new value produced beyond $v$ (French texts often write *pl*, Spanish ones *p*) |
| $c + v + s$ | the value of the product |
| $s' = s/v$ | the rate of surplus value, or rate of exploitation |
| $C = c + v$ | the capital advanced; in Volume III also the cost price $k$ |
| $p' = s/(c+v)$ | the rate of profit |
| $c/v$ | the value composition of capital; the *organic* composition when it follows changes in technique |

**Chapter numbers.** English editions (Moore and Aveling, and Penguin) divide Volume I into 33 chapters in eight parts, following Marx's French edition; this course uses those numbers. German editions, and translations made from them, have 25 chapters: chapters 4 to 6 of the English edition are chapter 4, chapters 7 to 25 are chapters 5 to 23, chapters 26 to 32 are chapter 24, and chapter 33 is chapter 25.

All quantities of value are measured in hours of socially necessary labour, or in money at a fixed value of money. The examples use round numbers and invented firms; Marx's own examples are quoted where they are famous.

**How it is organised.** Each part names the chapters it covers, explains the argument, works one example, and ends with what to remember. The exercises at the end have one computation per part, with answers. Parts 1 to 4 are the foundation; read them in order. Parts 9 to 12 can be read after Part 7.

**With Luma.** The *⚒️ Capital* test in Luma's *Economics* group has the same twelve parts as topics, at three levels, with written questions and computed ones (rates of surplus value and profit, the working day, values of products, prices of production, reproduction schemes, turnover, ground rent). A wrong answer opens the part of this course that explains it.

## Part 1 — The commodity

*Volume I, chapter 1.* Wealth in capitalist societies appears as "an immense accumulation of commodities", so Marx starts from the commodity and asks what makes things that are physically unlike exchangeable in definite proportions.

### Use value, exchange value, value

A commodity has two sides:

- a **use value**: it satisfies some need through its physical properties; use values are qualitatively different (a coat, linen, iron);
- an **exchange value**: the proportion in which it exchanges for others, for example 20 yards of linen = 1 coat.

If 20 yards of linen exchange for 1 coat, and also for 10 pounds of tea, the two equations express something common to linen, coats and tea that is not a physical property. Marx's answer is that they are all products of human labour, counted not as tailoring or weaving but as **abstract human labour** in general. That common substance is **value**; exchange value is the form in which value appears.

### Socially necessary labour time

The magnitude of value is the **socially necessary labour time** needed to produce the commodity: the time required "under the normal conditions of production, and with the average degree of skill and intensity prevalent at the time". Three consequences:

- A slow worker does not make a more valuable coat: individual labour counts only at the social average.
- When productivity rises, the value of each unit falls. When the power loom halved the labour needed to weave yarn into cloth, a hand-loom weaver's hour of work produced only half an hour of value.
- Labour that produces nothing anyone wants produces no value: a use value for others is a condition of value.

### The two-fold character of labour

Marx claimed this as his own discovery: the labour embodied in commodities is at once **concrete useful labour** (tailoring, weaving), which creates use values, and **abstract labour**, the expenditure of human labour-power as such, which creates value. Skilled labour counts as a multiple of simple labour. Classical economists (Smith, Ricardo) had a labour theory of value; they had not asked why labour takes the form of value at all.

### The value-form and money

Value cannot be seen in a single commodity; it appears only in the relation of exchange. Marx traces the development of this expression in four steps:

| Form | Expression | What it adds |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | 20 yards of linen = 1 coat | The linen's value is expressed in the coat's body: the linen is in the *relative* form, the coat in the *equivalent* form |
| Expanded | 20 yards of linen = 1 coat = 10 lb of tea = 40 lb of coffee = … | The linen's value can be expressed in every other commodity |
| General | 1 coat = 10 lb of tea = … = 20 yards of linen | All commodities express their value in one commodity, set apart as the general equivalent |
| Money | 20 yards of linen = 2 ounces of gold | The general equivalent is fixed by social custom in one commodity, gold |

Money is therefore not an invention or a convention added to exchange; it is the form value takes when exchange becomes general.

### The fetishism of commodities

In a society of independent private producers, people's labour becomes social only through the exchange of their products. Their social relations therefore appear to them as relations between things: prices move, markets decide, as if value were a natural property of goods. Marx calls this **fetishism**. It is not a mistake individuals could correct by thinking harder: it is how social labour actually works in this society. In a society with consciously planned production, his "community of free individuals", the relations of producers to their labour and its products would be, in his words, "perfectly simple and intelligible".

**Reading.** *Capital* I, chapter 1, is the hardest of the book; Marx himself warned that "every beginning is difficult". David Harvey's *Companion to Marx's Capital*, chapter 1, reads it section by section.

**What to remember.** A commodity is a use value and a value. Value is socially necessary abstract labour time, and appears only as exchange value. The money form is the end point of the value-form. Fetishism is social relations appearing as relations between things.

## Part 2 — Exchange and money

*Volume I, chapters 2 and 3.* Commodities do not go to market by themselves: their owners must recognise each other as private proprietors. Money grows out of this process and takes on several functions, each of which opens a possibility of breakdown.

### The functions of money

| Function | What money does | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Measure of value | Expresses the values of commodities as prices; only imaginary money is needed | A price is the money name of value; price and value can differ |
| Standard of price | A fixed weight of gold is the unit (pound, franc) | A convention of the state |
| Means of circulation | Mediates the exchange C–M–C | Coins and paper symbols can replace gold here |
| Hoard | Value withdrawn from circulation | The hoarder's "sisyphean" urge: money is qualitatively unlimited and quantitatively limited |
| Means of payment | Settles debts after the sale | Buyer and seller become debtor and creditor |
| World money | Settles balances between nations | Here money returns to its bullion form |

### The metamorphosis of commodities

The weaver sells linen for money and buys a Bible with it: **C–M–C**, selling in order to buy. Each sale is someone else's purchase, but nothing forces anyone to buy right after selling. The separation of sale and purchase in time and space is the **formal possibility of crises**: if many sellers hold on to their money, other commodities cannot be sold. Marx uses this against Say's law, the claim that supply creates its own demand.

### How much money circulates

For a given period, the quantity of money needed as means of circulation is

$$
M = \frac{\text{sum of prices}}{\text{velocity of money}}
$$

so that prices determine the quantity of money, not the reverse. Marx rejected the quantity theory of Hume and Ricardo, for which more money would raise prices. When money also serves as a means of payment, the formula becomes: sum of prices to be realised, minus sales on credit, plus payments falling due, minus payments that cancel each other, all divided by the velocity.

Example: in a month, commodities with prices of 1,200 are sold; 300 of these on credit; 400 of earlier debts fall due, of which 100 cancel out between the parties; each unit of money changes hands 4 times. Money required: $(1200 - 300 + 400 - 100)/4 = 300$.

When payments form a chain and one link fails, money is suddenly wanted as means of payment and nothing else will do: the **monetary crisis** Marx describes, when the cry that had called money a vain imagination becomes "money alone is a commodity!"

**Reading.** *Capital* I, chapters 2–3. Harvey, *Companion*, chapter 2.

**What to remember.** Money measures value, circulates commodities, is hoarded, pays debts and settles world balances. Prices determine the quantity of money in circulation. Selling without buying is the first possibility of crisis.

## Part 3 — From money to capital and surplus value

*Volume I, chapters 4 to 8.* Capital is money that grows. Marx explains where the growth comes from without assuming that anyone cheats: through the purchase of one special commodity, labour-power.

### The general formula of capital

Simple circulation is **C–M–C**: selling to buy, ending in a use value. Capital follows the reverse path, **M–C–M′**: buying in order to sell, where $M' = M + \Delta M$. An exchange of 100 for 100 would be pointless; the movement makes sense only if it ends with more money. That increment is **surplus value**. Because the end is again money, the same in kind as the start, the movement has no natural limit: capital is value in process, valorising itself.

### Where the increment cannot come from

Chapter 5 shows that surplus value cannot arise from exchange itself. If everyone sells 10 % above value, everyone also buys 10 % above value, and nothing changes. A cheat gains what his partner loses: the total value in society is unchanged. Surplus value must therefore arise in circulation and yet not in circulation.

### Labour-power

The solution is a commodity whose use value is to create value: **labour-power**, the capacity to work, as distinct from labour, its use. Its sale presupposes a worker who is "free in the double sense": free to sell their labour-power as their own property, and free of, that is without, any other means of producing a living. That situation is historical, not natural (Part 8).

The **value of labour-power** is the value of the means of subsistence needed to maintain the worker and raise the next generation, including training. Unlike other commodities, it contains a "historical and moral element": what counts as necessary depends on the country and on past struggles.

### The labour process and valorisation

Every labour process has three moments: purposeful activity, the object worked on, and the instruments of labour. Capitalist production is also a **valorisation process**:

Example (in hours of labour): a spinner works up raw cotton worth 20 hours and wears out machinery worth 4 hours. A day's labour-power costs 6 hours (the subsistence of a day takes 6 hours to produce). If the spinner works 6 hours, the yarn is worth $20 + 4 + 6 = 30$: the capitalist advanced 30 and gets 30. If the working day is 12 hours, the spinner works up twice the cotton and wears twice the machinery, and the yarn is worth $40 + 8 + 12 = 60$ for an outlay of $40 + 8 + 6 = 54$. The 6 extra hours are surplus value. The exchange of labour-power was an exchange of equivalents; the surplus comes from using it longer than the time that reproduces its value.

### Constant and variable capital

| | Constant capital $c$ | Variable capital $v$ |
|---|---|---|
| Bought as | Means of production: materials, energy, machines | Labour-power |
| In the product | Its value is transferred, no more: machinery by wear, materials whole | Its value is replaced, and a surplus value added |
| Changes magnitude? | No | Yes: it produces $v + s$ |

The distinction is about value creation, not physical form: a machine transfers value, only living labour creates new value. This is different from fixed and circulating capital (Part 9), a distinction about the speed of turnover.

**Reading.** *Capital* I, chapters 4–8. Harvey, *Companion*, chapters 3–4.

**What to remember.** Capital is M–C–M′. Surplus value cannot come from exchange; it comes from the use of labour-power beyond the time that reproduces its value. Constant capital transfers value; variable capital creates new value.

## Part 4 — The working day and absolute surplus value

*Volume I, chapters 9 to 11.* The rate of surplus value measures exploitation; the length of the working day is the first way to raise it, and the object of the oldest class struggle in industrial capitalism.

### The rate of surplus value

The working day splits into **necessary labour time**, in which the worker produces the equivalent of the value of their labour-power, and **surplus labour time**, in which they produce surplus value. The rate of surplus value is

$$
s' = \frac{s}{v} = \frac{\text{surplus labour}}{\text{necessary labour}}
$$

It is computed on variable capital alone, since only $v$ produces new value. Constant capital does not enter it.

Marx's example (chapter 9): a product of value $410c + 90v + 90s$. The rate of surplus value is $90/90 = 100\,\%$; with a 10-hour working day, necessary and surplus labour are 5 hours each. The rate of profit on the whole capital, $90/500 = 18\,\%$, hides that rate (Part 11).

### Absolute surplus value

With necessary labour fixed, surplus value grows by lengthening the working day: **absolute surplus value**. A day of 8 hours with 4 hours necessary labour gives $s' = 4/4 = 100\,\%$; at 12 hours, $8/4 = 200\,\%$.

### The working day

The working day has a physical maximum (sleep, food) and a moral one, but between those limits it is set by struggle: the capitalist buys a day of labour-power and claims its full use; the worker sells it and claims to keep the means of working again tomorrow. "Between equal rights force decides." Chapter 10 documents English factory reports: children working through the night, bakers' 18-hour days, deaths from overwork. The English **Factory Acts** fixed limits step by step: the Act of 1833 for children in textile mills, 1844 for women, the Ten Hours' Act of 1847, and the closing of the "relay system" loophole in 1850. Marx reads this legislation as won by workers' pressure, and also as in the interest of capital as a whole, which needs a working class that lasts.

### The mass of surplus value

The mass of surplus value is the rate times the variable capital: $S = s' \cdot V$, or the surplus value per worker times the number of workers. A fall in the number employed can be offset by a rise in the rate, but only within the limit of the working day: 24 hours less necessary labour.

**Reading.** *Capital* I, chapters 9–11. Harvey, *Companion*, chapter 5.

**What to remember.** $s' = s/v$ = surplus labour / necessary labour. Absolute surplus value comes from a longer working day. The length of the day is decided by struggle and law. $S = s' \cdot V$.

## Part 5 — Relative surplus value: cooperation, manufacture, machinery

*Volume I, chapters 12 to 18.* Once the working day is limited, capital raises surplus value by shortening necessary labour time: making the worker's means of subsistence cheaper. This drive explains the constant transformation of the labour process.

### The concept

If productivity rises in the industries producing wage goods (or the means of production used for them), the value of labour-power falls, and with the working day unchanged, surplus labour grows: **relative surplus value**. Example: a 10-hour day with 5 hours necessary labour gives $s' = 100\,\%$. If wage goods become 20 % cheaper, necessary labour falls to 4 hours and $s' = 6/4 = 150\,\%$, with the worker's consumption unchanged.

No capitalist aims at this directly. Each one introducing a better method produces below the social value of the commodity and pockets an **extra surplus value** until the method spreads. Competition, through the "external coercive laws" it imposes on every capitalist, then generalises the method and the extra gain disappears, leaving lower values behind.

### The stages of the labour process

| Form | Chapter | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| Cooperation | 13 | Many workers in one place under one capital: combined labour is more productive than the sum of individual labours; command becomes a function of capital |
| Manufacture | 14 | The division of the craft into detail operations; the "collective worker"; skill hierarchies; the worker crippled into a "detail labourer" |
| Machinery and large-scale industry | 15 | The tool passes to the machine; the worker becomes an appendage of the machine; science applied to production |

Machinery does not create value; it transfers its own. Its use pays for the capitalist when the labour it saves costs more than the machine's value transferred. Chapter 15 shows its effects: the employment of women and children, the prolonging of the working day to make the machine pay before it becomes obsolete, the **intensification** of labour once the day is limited, workers displaced and the destruction of crafts, but also the educational clauses of the Factory Acts and the need for workers able to change jobs.

### Formal and real subsumption

Marx distinguishes the **formal subsumption** of labour under capital, when capital takes over existing methods of work and lengthens the day, from **real subsumption**, when it reshapes the labour process itself through cooperation, manufacture and machinery. The terms appear briefly in chapter 16 and are developed in the manuscript "Results of the Immediate Process of Production", first published in 1933.

### Three variables

Chapter 17 combines the length of the working day, the **productivity** of labour and its **intensity**. A rise in productivity lowers the value of labour-power and raises $s'$ even if real wages rise somewhat; a rise in intensity is like a longer day compressed into the same hours.

**Reading.** *Capital* I, chapters 12–18. Harvey, *Companion*, chapters 6–8.

**What to remember.** Relative surplus value comes from cheaper wage goods. Extra surplus value drives each capitalist; competition spreads the method. Cooperation, manufacture, machinery: capital reshapes the labour process. Machines transfer value, they do not create it.

## Part 6 — Wages

*Volume I, chapters 19 to 22.* On the surface, workers seem to be paid for their labour. Marx shows how the wage form hides the division of the day into paid and unpaid labour.

### The value of labour-power as the "price of labour"

The expression "value of labour" is, for Marx, irrational: labour is the substance and measure of value and has no value itself. What is sold is labour-power. But the wage is paid after the work, per hour or per piece, so the whole working day appears paid. In serfdom, the unpaid labour on the lord's land is visible; in slavery, even paid labour appears unpaid; in wage labour, unpaid labour appears paid. Many illusions of economics and of law rest on this form.

### Time wages and piece wages

- **Time wages**: the price of an hour of labour is the daily value of labour-power divided by the length of the day. If the day is lengthened without a rise in the daily wage, the hourly price falls.
- **Piece wages**: a converted form of time wages, the form of wages "most in harmony with the capitalist mode of production". The rate per piece is set from the output of an average day; the wage itself controls quality and intensity, invites workers to work longer and harder, and allows subcontracting.

### National differences

Comparing wages between countries means comparing the value of labour-power together with the intensity and productivity of labour. A country with more intense and productive labour may have higher money wages and yet a higher rate of surplus value.

**Reading.** *Capital* I, chapters 19–22.

**What to remember.** The wage appears as the price of labour and so hides unpaid labour. Piece wages are disguised time wages that intensify work. International wage comparisons must account for intensity and productivity.

## Part 7 — The accumulation of capital

*Volume I, chapters 23 to 25.* Surplus value turned back into capital makes capital grow. Marx shows what this does to the relation between capital and workers and to the size and condition of the working class.

### Simple reproduction

Even if all surplus value is consumed, repetition changes the picture. A capital of 1,000 producing 200 of surplus value a year, all consumed by its owner, has after five years been consumed in value; what remains is capitalised surplus value, unpaid labour of others. And each round reproduces the relation itself: capital on one side, workers without means of production on the other, who must sell their labour-power again.

### Expanded reproduction

When part of the surplus value is added to capital, production grows. The laws of property based on the exchange of equivalents turn into their opposite: the capitalist appropriates the unpaid labour of others and uses it to buy more labour. Political economy's "abstinence" theory of profit is mocked here. For the capitalist as a personification of capital: "Accumulate, accumulate! That is Moses and the prophets!"

### The composition of capital

| Composition | Measured as | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Technical | Mass of means of production per worker | The physical ratio |
| Value | $c/v$ | The ratio of values |
| Organic | $c/v$ as it follows the technical composition | The value composition when it reflects changes in technique |

Accumulation tends to raise the organic composition: each worker sets in motion more means of production. The demand for labour therefore grows more slowly than capital, and falls relative to it.

### The industrial reserve army

The result is a **relative surplus population**, the industrial reserve army, which capital creates by its own accumulation, not by population growth: Marx turns against Malthus here. The reserve army regulates wages: in booms it is absorbed and wages rise; in slumps it swells and presses on the employed. Marx distinguishes its forms:

| Form | Who |
|---|---|
| Floating | Workers repelled and attracted by industry with the cycle |
| Latent | Rural populations ready to move into industry |
| Stagnant | Irregular, casual work at the bottom of conditions |
| Pauperism | Those unable to work, the "hospital of the active labour army" |

### The general law

The greater the social wealth and the energy of its growth, the greater the reserve army and the mass of poverty relative to it: "accumulation of wealth at one pole is, therefore, at the same time accumulation of misery […] at the opposite pole". Whether this is a law of absolute impoverishment or of relative deprivation is one of the oldest debates: most readers today, Harvey included, read it as a relative law with absolute effects in parts of the working class and of the world.

**Reading.** *Capital* I, chapters 23–25. Harvey, *Companion*, chapters 9–10.

**What to remember.** Simple reproduction reproduces the capital relation. Accumulation raises the organic composition. Capital makes its own reserve army, which regulates wages. Wealth and poverty accumulate together.

## Part 8 — So-called primitive accumulation

*Volume I, chapters 26 to 33.* Capitalist accumulation presupposes capitalists with money and workers without means of production. How did that starting point come about? Not, Marx answers, by the thrift of some and the laziness of others, as political economy tells the story, but by force.

### The expropriation of the producers

The core is the separation of direct producers from their means of production, above all the land. In England: the dissolution of feudal retinues, the enclosure of commons, the seizure of Church lands in the Reformation, the "clearing of estates", up to the Highland Clearances of the 19th century. Those driven off the land became the first proletarians.

### Disciplining a working class

The expropriated did not become wage workers by themselves. **"Bloody legislation"** against vagabondage, from the 15th century on, punished those without work with whipping, branding, slavery or death, and statutes fixed maximum wages. The state was used to create and discipline a working class.

### Capital's other origins

The capitalist farmer grew from tenancy and the price revolution of the 16th century; the home market from the destruction of rural domestic industry. Industrial capital drew on the **colonial system**, the slave trade, the public debt, modern taxation and protectionism: "capital comes dripping from head to foot, from every pore, with blood and dirt".

### The historical tendency

Chapter 32 sketches the whole movement: private property based on the owner's own labour is expropriated by capitalist private property; capital centralises; the capitalist "integument is burst asunder" and "the expropriators are expropriated". Chapter 33 uses the colonies, where land was free, to show that capital is a social relation: Wakefield discovered that settlers could not be made to work for wages while they could take land.

### Later readings

Rosa Luxemburg (1913) argued that capital needs non-capitalist surroundings to keep accumulating. David Harvey's "accumulation by dispossession" extends the idea to privatisation, debt and enclosures today. Postcolonial readings, gathered in *Marxism, Modernity and Postcolonial Studies*, question how far Marx's English model fits colonial histories.

**Reading.** *Capital* I, chapters 26–33. Harvey, *Companion*, chapter 11.

**What to remember.** Capitalism begins with the separation of producers from the means of production, achieved by force. The state disciplines the new working class. Colonialism, slavery and public debt fed early capital.

## Part 9 — The circuits and turnover of capital

*Volume II, parts 1 and 2.* Volume I studied production; Volume II studies how capital passes through its different forms and how long that takes.

### Three circuits

Industrial capital passes through three forms, money capital, productive capital and commodity capital, in a circuit:

$$
M - C\,(LP + MP) \ldots P \ldots C' - M'
$$

Money buys labour-power $LP$ and means of production $MP$; production $P$ yields commodities $C'$ containing surplus value; their sale returns $M'$. The same movement can be seen from three starting points:

| Circuit | Formula | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Money capital | $M \ldots M'$ | The aim: more money; the viewpoint of the money owner |
| Productive capital | $P \ldots P$ | Continuous production and reproduction |
| Commodity capital | $C' \ldots C'$ | The product must be sold; the viewpoint of social reproduction |

A capital must exist in all three forms at once, in different portions, for production to be continuous; any interruption, a stock that does not sell, stops the whole.

### Time of production and circulation, costs of circulation

**Turnover time** is production time plus circulation time. Production time can exceed working time (wine fermenting, crops growing). Circulation time is the time to buy and sell. **Costs of circulation** that only change the form of value, buying, selling, bookkeeping, create no value and are paid out of surplus value; storage and transport, in so far as they are needed for use, are productive.

### Fixed and circulating capital

| | Fixed capital | Circulating capital |
|---|---|---|
| Transfers its value | Gradually, over many turnovers | Entirely, in each turnover |
| Examples | Buildings, machines | Raw materials, energy, wages |

This distinction is about turnover; constant and variable is about value creation. Variable capital is always circulating; constant capital can be either. Marx criticises Smith and Ricardo for mixing the two, which hides the source of surplus value.

### The annual rate of surplus value

The number of turnovers in a year is $n = $ year / turnover time. A variable capital that turns over $n$ times a year is used $n$ times: with the rate of surplus value $s'$, the **annual rate of surplus value** is

$$
s'_{\text{annual}} = s' \cdot n
$$

Marx's example: a variable capital of 500 turning over 10 times a year at $s' = 100\,\%$ yields 5,000 of surplus value a year, an annual rate of 1,000 %, while a capital of 5,000 turning over once yields 5,000 at 100 %. The rate of exploitation is the same; the faster turnover means less capital must be advanced.

**Reading.** *Capital* II, chapters 1–17. Harvey, *Companion to Marx's Capital, Volume 2*, chapters 1–3 and 8–9.

**What to remember.** Capital circulates as money, production and commodities. Turnover time = production time + circulation time. Fixed and circulating is about turnover; constant and variable about value. Annual rate = $s' \cdot n$.

## Part 10 — The reproduction of the total social capital

*Volume II, part 3.* How can the whole economy reproduce itself, each capital finding buyers and the means of production it needs? Marx answers with two departments, inspired by Quesnay's *Tableau économique*.

### Two departments

- **Department I** produces means of production.
- **Department II** produces means of consumption.

Each product is $c + v + s$. In **simple reproduction**, all surplus value is consumed. Department I's product, means of production, must replace the constant capital of both departments; Department II's product, consumer goods, must feed the workers and capitalists of both. The exchange between departments balances when

$$
\text{I}(v + s) = \text{II}\,c
$$

Marx's example: I: $4000c + 1000v + 1000s = 6000$; II: $2000c + 500v + 500s = 3000$. Department I keeps 4,000 of its product to replace its own $c$, and sells 2,000 to Department II for II's $c$; Department II keeps 1,000 for its own workers and capitalists and sells 2,000 of consumer goods to Department I. $\text{I}(1000 + 1000) = \text{II}\,2000$: the scheme balances.

### Expanded reproduction

If part of the surplus value is accumulated, Department I must produce more means of production than both departments use up:

$$
\text{I}(v + s) > \text{II}\,c
$$

Marx's starting scheme: I: $4000c + 1000v + 1000s$; II: $1500c + 750v + 750s$. Here $\text{I}(v+s) = 2000 > \text{II}\,c = 1500$: 500 of means of production are available for accumulation.

Example of one year's accumulation (as in Marx's first scheme): Department I accumulates half its surplus value, 500, keeping its composition $c/v = 4$: 400 more $c$ and 100 more $v$. Next year it starts with $4400c + 1100v$; at $s' = 100\,\%$ it produces $4400c + 1100v + 1100s = 6600$.

### Why the schemes matter

The schemes show that balanced reproduction is possible, but only under exact proportions that nobody plans. Their reading has divided Marxists: Tugan-Baranovsky concluded that capitalism could grow without limit if proportions were kept; Rosa Luxemburg that surplus value could not be realised without non-capitalist buyers; Bukharin and later Grossman answered both. The schemes are also an ancestor of input–output analysis and of two-sector growth models.

**Reading.** *Capital* II, chapters 18–21. Harvey, *Companion, Volume 2*, chapters 10–11.

**What to remember.** Department I makes means of production, II means of consumption. Simple reproduction: $\text{I}(v+s) = \text{II}\,c$. Expanded reproduction: $\text{I}(v+s) > \text{II}\,c$. Balance is possible but unplanned.

## Part 11 — Profit, prices of production and the falling rate of profit

*Volume III, parts 1 to 3.* Volume III returns from the inner structure to the surface: profit, competition, prices. It explains why prices do not match values, and why the rate of profit tends to fall.

### Cost price and profit

For the capitalist, what a commodity costs is the **cost price** $k = c + v$; its value is $k + s$. Surplus value, related to the whole capital advanced, appears as **profit**, the fruit of the capital as a whole rather than of labour alone. The rate of profit is

$$
p' = \frac{s}{c + v} = s' \cdot \frac{v}{c + v}
$$

Since $c > 0$, the rate of profit is always lower than the rate of surplus value. Example: $80c + 20v + 20s$ gives $s' = 100\,\%$ and $p' = 20\,\%$.

### The general rate of profit and prices of production

Capitals of equal size with different compositions produce different masses of surplus value at the same rate of exploitation. Marx's table (chapter 9), with $s' = 100\,\%$:

| Capital | Composition | Surplus value | Rate of profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | $80c + 20v$ | 20 | 20 % |
| II | $70c + 30v$ | 30 | 30 % |
| III | $60c + 40v$ | 40 | 40 % |
| IV | $85c + 15v$ | 15 | 15 % |
| V | $95c + 5v$ | 5 | 5 % |

If commodities sold at their values, capital would flow from low-profit branches to high-profit ones. **Competition** between capitals equalises the rates to a **general rate of profit**: total surplus value over total capital, here $110/500 = 22\,\%$. Commodities then sell at **prices of production**:

$$
\text{price of production} = k + \bar{p}' \cdot C
$$

where $k$ is the cost price of the commodity and $C$ the capital advanced. Capital I's product sells at $100 + 22 = 122$ instead of 120; capital V's at 122 instead of 105. Prices above value in branches of high composition, below value in branches of low composition; in Marx's table, total prices equal total values (610) and total profit equals total surplus value (110). Surplus value is produced in each branch but distributed by competition in proportion to capital.

### The transformation problem

In Marx's table the inputs, $c$ and $v$, are still counted at values, though they are themselves bought at prices of production. Marx noted this. Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz (1907) corrected the calculation and showed that, in general, total prices cannot equal total values and total profit equal total surplus value at the same time. Ian Steedman's *Marx after Sraffa* (1977) argued that prices and the profit rate can be computed from physical data without values at all. Responses include the "New Interpretation" (Duménil, Foley), the temporal single-system interpretation (Kliman), and value-form readings (Heinrich) for which values are not quantities computed before prices. The debate is open; the *Dictionary of Marxist Thought* and the *Critical Companion to Contemporary Marxism* summarise the positions.

### The law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall

Write $p' = s'/(c/v + 1)$. If the organic composition $c/v$ rises (Part 7) and $s'$ is constant, $p'$ falls. Example: at $s' = 100\,\%$, a composition of $4$ gives $p' = 1/5 = 20\,\%$; a composition of $9$ gives $1/10 = 10\,\%$. Marx calls this a law of a *tendency*, because of **counteracting influences** (chapter 14):

1. A rising intensity of exploitation (higher $s'$).
2. Wages pressed below the value of labour-power.
3. The cheapening of the elements of constant capital, which slows the rise of $c/v$ in value.
4. Relative over-population, which keeps labour-intensive branches alive.
5. Foreign trade.
6. The increase of share capital, which takes only interest.

The contradictions of the law (chapter 15) are played out in crises: capital overaccumulated relative to the profit it can make, devaluation and destruction of capital, then a new start. Whether the law holds is disputed: Nobuo Okishio (1961) showed that a cost-reducing technique adopted at given real wages cannot lower the profit rate; supporters answer with rising real wages, fixed capital and historical data.

**Reading.** *Capital* III, chapters 1–15. Harvey, *Marx, Capital and the Madness of Economic Reason*, chapters 4–5 (devaluation, prices and values).

**What to remember.** $p' = s/(c+v) < s'$. Competition equalises profit rates; commodities sell at prices of production $k + \bar{p}' C$. The transformation problem is a real debate. The rate of profit tends to fall as $c/v$ rises, against counteracting influences.

## Part 12 — Merchant capital, interest, rent and revenues

*Volume III, parts 4 to 7.* Surplus value produced in industry is shared among several claimants: merchants, money-lenders, landowners. These parts explain how, and why each share appears to have its own source.

### Merchant capital

Commercial capital specialises in buying and selling. It creates no value, but by shortening circulation time it saves industrial capital, and it shares in the general rate of profit: industrial capitalists sell to merchants below the price of production, merchants sell at it. The general rate of profit is then total surplus value over industrial plus commercial capital. **Money-dealing capital** handles payments, cash and exchange.

### Interest-bearing capital and credit

Money lent as capital is a commodity of a special kind: its use value is to function as capital and produce profit. The lender's circuit is **M–M′**, money that seems to breed money, where the relations of capital take "their most externalised and most fetish-like form". **Interest** is a part of profit paid to the lender; the rest is **profit of enterprise**. There is no "natural" rate of interest: it is set by the supply of and demand for loan capital, between zero and the whole profit.

The credit system speeds circulation, centralises capital in **joint-stock companies**, which Marx calls "the abolition of capital as private property within the framework of capitalist production itself", and creates **fictitious capital**: titles to future income, such as shares and government bonds, priced by capitalising that income. A bond paying 50 a year is worth $50 / 0.05 = 1000$ when the interest rate is 5 %, whatever was spent when it was issued. Credit also drives speculation and crises. Engels's edition of Part 5 is the most heavily reworked of Volume III.

### Ground rent

Landowners take part of surplus value as **rent**, paid by capitalist farmers for the use of land.

- **Differential rent I** comes from differences in fertility or location. The price of the product is regulated by the price of production on the worst land in use; better lands produce the same product at lower cost and pay the difference as rent.
- **Differential rent II** comes from successive investments of capital on the same land with different productivity.
- **Absolute rent** is paid even on the worst land, because landed property is a barrier to investment.

Example of differential rent I (as in Marx's table, chapter 39): four lands A, B, C, D each take a capital of 50 and, with an average profit of 10, have a price of production of 60. They yield 1, 2, 3 and 4 quarters of wheat. The worst land A regulates the price: 60 per quarter. Then B sells 2 quarters for 120 and pays a rent of 60; C pays 120; D pays 180.

The **price of land** is capitalised rent: rent / rate of interest. Land yielding a rent of 200 at an interest rate of 4 % sells for 5,000.

### The trinity formula

On the surface of society, revenues seem to have three independent sources: capital yields profit (or interest), land yields rent, labour yields wages. Marx calls this the **trinity formula** and shows that all three revenues are parts of the value created by labour. Volume III ends with an unfinished chapter on classes, cut off after a page.

**Reading.** *Capital* III, chapters 16–52. Harvey, *Companion, Volume 2*, chapters 4–7 on merchants' capital, interest and credit.

**What to remember.** Merchants share in profit without producing value. Interest is part of profit; its rate has no natural level. Fictitious capital is capitalised future income. Differential rent comes from the worst land setting the price. Price of land = rent / interest rate.

## Exercises

One computation per part, then questions to check yourself. Answers follow.

### Problems

- [ ] **Problem 1 (Part 1).** A shoemaker's average time per pair is 4 hours. A new machine halves the socially necessary time. How much value does a slow workshop, still taking 4 hours, produce per pair after the change?
- [ ] **Problem 2 (Part 2).** In a week, commodities with prices of 2,000 are sold; 500 on credit. Payments of 600 fall due, of which 200 cancel out. Money turns over 5 times. How much money is needed?
- [ ] **Problem 3 (Part 3).** A worker uses up materials worth 30 hours and machinery worth 6 hours a day. Labour-power costs 5 hours a day; the working day is 10 hours. What are $c$, $v$, $s$ and the value of the product?
- [ ] **Problem 4 (Part 4).** A capital of $360c + 90v$ yields a product worth 630. Find $s$, $s'$ and the surplus labour in a 9-hour day.
- [ ] **Problem 5 (Part 5).** Working day 12 hours, necessary labour 6 hours. Wage goods become 25 % cheaper. Find the new necessary labour and the new $s'$.
- [ ] **Problem 6 (Part 6).** The daily value of labour-power is 60, for a 10-hour day. The day is lengthened to 12 hours with the same daily wage. What happens to the price of an hour of labour?
- [ ] **Problem 7 (Part 7).** A capital of $800c + 200v$ accumulates 100, keeping $c/v$. How is the 100 split, and how many more workers (at a wage of 2 each) are hired?
- [ ] **Problem 8 (Part 8).** A colony offers free land to every settler. Using chapter 33, explain why a capitalist who brings money, tools and workers from Europe can still find himself "without a servant to make his bed".
- [ ] **Problem 9 (Part 9).** A variable capital of 1,000 turns over 4 times a year at $s' = 150\,\%$. Find the surplus value produced in a year and the annual rate.
- [ ] **Problem 10 (Part 10).** Department I: $3000c + 750v + 750s$. Department II: $c = 1500$. Is simple reproduction possible? If not, by how much do the two sides differ?
- [ ] **Problem 11 (Part 11).** Three capitals of 100: $60c + 40v$, $80c + 20v$, $90c + 10v$, at $s' = 100\,\%$, all $c$ used up. Find the general rate of profit and the price of production of each product.
- [ ] **Problem 12 (Part 12).** Four lands each need a capital of 100 with an average profit of 20, and yield 2, 3, 4 and 6 tonnes. Find the regulating price per tonne and the rent on each land.

### Check yourself

1. Why is the value of a commodity not set by the time its own producer took?
2. Why can surplus value not come from buying cheap and selling dear?
3. What is the difference between labour and labour-power?
4. Why does constant capital not enter the rate of surplus value?
5. How can a single capitalist gain from a new machine before it lowers the value of labour-power?
6. Why does the wage form hide unpaid labour?
7. What creates the industrial reserve army, according to Marx, and what does it do?
8. Why is "primitive" accumulation not explained by saving?
9. What is the difference between fixed capital and constant capital?
10. What condition must hold between the departments for simple reproduction?
11. Why do commodities not sell at their values in Volume III?
12. Why is the price of land not a value?

### Answers

**Problems.**

1. 2 hours per pair: value is set by the new socially necessary time; the slow workshop's extra 2 hours create no value.
2. $(2000 - 500 + 600 - 200)/5 = 380$.
3. $c = 30 + 6 = 36$, $v = 5$, $s = 10 - 5 = 5$; value $36 + 5 + 5 = 46$ hours.
4. $s = 630 - 450 = 180$; $s' = 180/90 = 200\,\%$; surplus labour is two thirds of the day: 6 of 9 hours.
5. Necessary labour $6 \times 0.75 = 4.5$ hours; $s' = 7.5/4.5 = 166.7\,\%$ (it was 100 %).
6. It falls from 6 to 5 per hour: the hourly price is the daily value divided by the length of the day.
7. $c/v = 4$: 80 to $c$, 20 to $v$; 10 more workers.
8. The workers can leave and farm their own land: as long as land is free, they are not separated from the means of production, so their labour-power is not a commodity for long. Capital is a social relation, not a stock of money and tools.
9. $1000 \times 1.5 \times 4 = 6000$; annual rate $150\,\% \times 4 = 600\,\%$.
10. $\text{I}(v+s) = 1500 = \text{II}\,c$: simple reproduction is possible, the sides are equal.
11. Total $s = 40 + 20 + 10 = 70$ on 300: $\bar{p}' = 23.3\,\%$; each price of production is $100 + 23.3 = 123.3$ (values were 140, 120, 110).
12. Regulating price from the worst land: $120/2 = 60$ per tonne. Rents: 0, $180 - 120 = 60$, $240 - 120 = 120$, $360 - 120 = 240$.

**Check yourself.**

1. Value is socially necessary labour time: the average conditions of the time, not individual effort.
2. In exchange one party's gain is another's loss; the total value in society does not change.
3. Labour-power is the capacity to work, sold for a day; labour is its use, which can produce more value than labour-power costs.
4. Constant capital only transfers its value; the rate measures new value relative to the labour-power that produced it.
5. Producing below the social value, he sells at the social value and pockets an extra surplus value until the method spreads.
6. The wage is paid per hour or per piece, after the work, so the whole day appears paid.
7. Accumulation itself, as a rising organic composition reduces the demand for labour relative to capital; it regulates wages.
8. It was the forcible separation of producers from the means of production: enclosures, expropriation, colonial plunder, laws against vagabonds.
9. Fixed capital transfers its value over many turnovers (a turnover distinction); constant capital transfers value without creating new value (a value distinction). Raw materials are constant but circulating.
10. $\text{I}(v + s) = \text{II}\,c$.
11. Competition equalises profit rates between branches with different compositions, so commodities sell at prices of production.
12. Land is not a product of labour; its price is capitalised rent, a claim on surplus value.
